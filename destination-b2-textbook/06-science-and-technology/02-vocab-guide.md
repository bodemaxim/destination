# Unit 6 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### artificial / false

**Artificial** means made by people, not found in nature (artificial light, an artificial heart). **False** means not true, not real, or meant to deceive (a false statement, false teeth). Something artificial is not always a lie.

- **artificial** — The lab uses artificial light so the plants grow at the same speed all year.
- **false** — The article was taken down because it contained several false claims about the vaccine.

### natural / physical

**Natural** means existing in nature, not made by people. **Physical** refers to the body, or to things you can touch, as opposed to mental or virtual.

- **natural** — This soap is made from natural oils and contains no artificial colouring at all.
- **physical** — After the accident he needed months of physical therapy before he could walk.

### true / accurate

**True** means in agreement with reality; a fact can be true or false. **Accurate** means exact and without error, especially of measurements, data or descriptions.

- **true** — It is true that water boils at a lower temperature high up in the mountains.
- **accurate** — We need more accurate data before we can publish the results of the trial.

### method / way

A **method** is an organised, often scientific or technical, procedure. A **way** is a more general word for how you do something.

- **method** — Their method of testing the new battery was later copied by other laboratories.
- **way** — There must be a better way to charge the phone without using so much cable.

### engine / machine / motor

An **engine** converts fuel or heat into movement, especially in a car or plane. A **machine** is any device that uses power to do work. A **motor** is usually a smaller device, often electric, that produces motion.

- **engine** — The plane’s engine failed shortly after take-off, but the pilots landed safely.
- **machine** — This machine can print a three-dimensional model in less than two hours.
- **motor** — The electric motor in the robot is almost silent when it moves across the floor.

### aim / cause / reason

An **aim** is what you want to achieve. A **cause** is what makes something happen. A **reason** is why someone does something, or why something is true.

- **aim** — The aim of the project is to make drinking water cheaper in dry regions.
- **cause** — Scientists are still looking for the cause of the sudden rise in temperature.
- **reason** — The reason we delayed the launch was a problem with the fuel pump.

### estimate / calculate

**Estimate** means to make an approximate judgement, often without exact figures. **Calculate** means to work out an exact number, usually with maths.

- **estimate** — We estimate that the new satellite will last about twelve years in orbit.
- **calculate** — She calculated the exact amount of energy the solar panel would produce.

### electric / electronic

**Electric** uses electricity as power (an electric car, an electric kettle). **Electronic** uses electrical circuits and chips to process information (an electronic dictionary, electronic mail).

- **electric** — They replaced the old gas cooker with a new electric one last winter.
- **electronic** — Electronic tickets are sent to your phone as soon as you pay online.

### invent / discover

You **invent** something that did not exist before (a machine, a process). You **discover** something that was already there (a planet, a law of nature, a fact).

- **invent** — She invented a cheap filter that removes plastic from river water.
- **discover** — They discovered a new species of insect in the rainforest last summer.

### research / experiment

**Research** is the organised study of a subject, often over a long time. An **experiment** is a practical test you carry out to check an idea.

- **research** — Her research into sleep patterns has been published in several journals.
- **experiment** — In this experiment we heat the liquid and record the temperature every minute.

### progress / development

**Progress** is improvement or movement forward. **Development** is the process of growing, changing, or creating something new (a product, a skill, a child’s development).

- **progress** — The team has made real progress on the battery, but it still overheats.
- **development** — The development of the vaccine took less than a year of laboratory work.

### modern / new

**Modern** means of the present time, using current methods or design. **New** means recently made, found or started; a new idea is not always modern in style.

- **modern** — Modern telescopes can photograph galaxies that are billions of light years away.
- **new** — They installed a new server yesterday, and the old one is already in storage.

### industry / factory

**Industry** is a whole type of business (the car industry) or manufacturing in general. A **factory** is a building where goods are produced.

- **industry** — The computer industry has grown faster than anyone predicted twenty years ago.
- **factory** — The factory outside town produces parts for electric cars and employs four hundred people.

### award / reward

An **award** is a prize given for achievement (an award for science). A **reward** is something you get in return for help or good work, often money.

- **award** — She won a national award for her work on cheap solar panels for schools.
- **reward** — The company offered a reward to anyone who could fix the software bug.

### take place / occur

**Take place** is used for planned events. **Occur** is more formal and is used for things that happen, including problems and natural events; it is not usually used for organised meetings.

- **take place** — The conference will take place in Berlin in March, with scientists from twenty countries.
- **occur** — Power cuts occur most often in winter, when demand for electricity is at its highest.

## Phrasal verbs

- **break down** — The scanner broke down in the middle of the test and we lost an hour of work.
- **carry out** — They carried out the experiment twice to make sure the results were not a mistake.
- **come off** — If the plan comes off, rural schools will finally have a decent internet connection.
- **come on** — His programming has come on a lot since he started the evening computer class.
- **come up with** — She came up with a simple design that used far less plastic than the old model.
- **cut off** — The company cut off their internet when they failed to pay the bill for three months.
- **find out** — We need to find out whether the software can run on older machines as well.
- **give off** — The old monitor gives off a lot of heat, even when you are only reading email.
- **narrow down** — After the first tests we narrowed down the list of possible faults to three.
- **plug in** — Plug in the printer at the back of the computer before you switch them both on.
- **put through** — Could you put me through to the engineer who installed the new server, please?
- **turn into** — That small start-up has turned into one of the biggest phone companies in Europe.
- **turn off** — Turn off the Bunsen burner as soon as you have finished heating the liquid.
- **work out** — I cannot work out why the numbers in this table do not add up correctly.

## Phrases and collocations

### attempt

- **make an attempt at sth** — They made an attempt at a cheaper battery, but it still overheated too quickly.
- **make an attempt at doing** — She made an attempt at explaining the formula, but most of us were lost.
- **make an attempt to do** — The lab made an attempt to copy the results, and this time they matched.
- **attempt to do** — Scientists attempted to grow the plants without any soil at all.
- **in an attempt to do** — In an attempt to save energy, the office turns off every screen at night.

### average

- **on average** — On average, a smartphone battery lasts about a day with normal use.

### beginning

- **in the beginning** — In the beginning nobody believed the theory, but the data later supported it.
- **at the beginning (of sth)** — At the beginning of the lecture she explained how a microchip is made.
- **beginning with** — List the planets beginning with the one closest to the sun.

### bottom

- **at/on the bottom (of sth)** — The serial number is printed at the bottom of the machine, under the cable.

### cause

- **be the cause of sth** — A loose wire was the cause of the fire in the old laboratory.
- **find the cause of sth** — It took the engineers a week to find the cause of the system crash.
- **look for the cause of sth** — They are still looking for the cause of the strange signal from space.

### conclusion

- **come to the conclusion (that)** — We came to the conclusion that the sample had been contaminated.
- **reach the conclusion (that)** — The team reached the conclusion that the software, not the hardware, was at fault.
- **in conclusion** — In conclusion, the new method is slower but far more accurate than the old one.

### experiment

- **do an experiment (on sth)** — We did an experiment on metal fatigue using three different types of steel.
- **perform an experiment (on sth)** — They performed an experiment on mice before they tested the drug on people.
- **carry out an experiment (on sth)** — Students carried out an experiment on plant growth under red and blue light.
- **experiment with sth** — The company is experimenting with recycled plastic in its new phone cases.
- **experiment with doing** — She has been experimenting with teaching physics through short online videos.

### fact

- **in fact** — The battery looks small; in fact it holds more charge than last year’s model.
- **as a matter of fact** — As a matter of fact, the first email was sent more than fifty years ago.
- **the fact (of the matter) is (that)** — The fact is that we still cannot store wind energy cheaply enough.
- **face the facts** — We have to face the facts: this prototype will never be safe enough to sell.

### introduction

- **with the introduction of sth** — With the introduction of 5G, download speeds in the city improved overnight.
- **an introduction to sth** — The first chapter is an introduction to the basic laws of electricity.
- **an introduction to sb** — After an introduction to the head of research, we were shown around the lab.

### phone call

- **make a phone call** — I need to make a phone call to the supplier about the missing parts.
- **receive a phone call** — She received a phone call from NASA offering her a place on the project.
- **get a phone call** — He got a phone call at midnight because the server in Tokyo had crashed.

### photo(graph)

- **take a photo (of sth)** — Take a photo of the circuit board before you remove any of the chips.
- **take a photo (of sb)** — The journalist took a photo of the inventor standing beside the new telescope.

### research

- **carry out research (on/into sth)** — They are carrying out research on how sleep affects memory in teenagers.
- **do research (on/into sth)** — She did research into cheap ways of cleaning oil from seawater.

## Word patterns

### cause

- **cause sth** — A single coding error caused the delay of the whole satellite launch.
- **cause sth to do** — The heat caused the metal to expand and crack the glass container.

### consider

- **consider sth** — The board will consider the new design at their meeting on Friday.
- **consider doing** — We are considering using hydrogen instead of petrol in the next prototype.
- **consider if/whether** — Consider whether the sample is large enough before you publish the graph.
- **consider sb for sth** — They considered her for the lead role on the Mars rover software team.
- **consider it strange, etc (for sb to do)** — I consider it strange for a lab to publish results without any raw data.

### discuss

- **discuss sth** — We discussed the results for an hour and still could not agree on a cause.
- **discuss doing** — They discussed moving the whole experiment to a larger laboratory next year.
- **discuss sth with sb** — You should discuss the safety rules with every new student in the lab.

### explain

- **explain that** — She explained that the machine must cool down before anyone opens the lid.
- **explain sth** — Can you explain this graph in simpler language for the school magazine?
- **explain sth to sb** — He explained the process to the visitors while they watched through the glass.

### intend

- **intend to do** — They intend to test the new chip on a thousand phones before they sell it.
- **intend doing** — We intend starting the trial in June, as soon as the ethics form is signed.

### know

- **know (about) sth** — Do you know about the update that fixes the bug in last week’s release?
- **know (about) doing** — He knows about writing code, but he has never built a physical robot.
- **know of sb** — I know of a technician who can repair this model without replacing the board.
- **be known as sth** — Ada Lovelace is known as one of the first computer programmers in history.

### look

- **look at sth** — Look at the data on the screen before you change any of the settings.
- **look for sb** — We are looking for an engineer who has already worked with hydrogen cells.
- **look forward to sth** — The students are looking forward to the trip to the science museum.
- **look forward to doing** — I look forward to seeing the first pictures from the new space telescope.

### manage

- **manage to do** — Against the odds, they managed to restart the server before the shops opened.

### plan

- **plan sth** — The university is planning a new laboratory for research into clean energy.
- **plan to do** — We plan to publish the full dataset as soon as the review is complete.

### possible

- **possible (for sb) to do** — It is now possible to sequence a genome in a single afternoon.
- **possible for sb to do** — It is possible for students to run the simulation on an ordinary laptop.
- **find sth possible** — She found the new method possible after a week of practice in the lab.
- **find it impossible to do** — They found it impossible to copy the results with the old equipment.

### result

- **result of sth** — The crash was the result of a tiny fault in the braking software.
- **result of doing** — The burn was the result of leaving the laser on for too long.
- **result in sth** — A small error in the code resulted in a delay of three whole months.
- **result in (your) doing** — Poor ventilation resulted in the team working in short shifts only.
- **result from sth** — Most of the damage resulted from the flood, not from the power cut itself.
- **result from doing** — The improvement resulted from changing the order of the chemical steps.
- **as a result of sth** — As a result of the new law, every new phone must be easier to repair.

### wonder

- **wonder about sth** — I still wonder about the long-term effects of so much screen time.
- **wonder about doing** — She wondered about studying robotics instead of pure mathematics.
- **wonder if/whether** — I wonder if this battery will still hold a charge after five years.
- **wonder why** — Scientists still wonder why the probe stopped sending data after three days.
