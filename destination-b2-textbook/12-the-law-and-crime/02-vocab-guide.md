# Unit 12 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### proof / evidence

**Proof** is something that shows a fact is definitely true; it settles the question. **Evidence** is information that supports a claim (fingerprints, CCTV, a witness statement) but may not be enough on its own. In court you present evidence; you may or may not have proof.

- **proof** — The video is proof that he was in the shop when the money disappeared.
- **evidence** — The police found evidence at the scene, but it was not enough to charge anyone.

### suspect / arrest / charge

To **suspect** someone is to think they may have committed a crime. To **arrest** them is to take them into police custody. To **charge** them is to formally accuse them of a specific offence so the case can go to court.

- **suspect** — Officers suspect a former employee, but they have not named him in public.
- **arrest** — They arrested her at dawn and took her to the station for questioning.
- **charge** — The prosecutor charged both men with theft after the investigation ended.

### suspect / accused

A **suspect** is someone the police think might be responsible, often before a formal charge. The **accused** is the person facing a charge in court. A suspect may never become the accused if there is too little evidence.

- **suspect** — The main suspect left the country before the detectives could interview him.
- **accused** — The accused sat quietly while the lawyer read the list of charges.

### decision / verdict

A **decision** is a general choice made by a person, a judge, or an organisation. A **verdict** is the jury’s (or court’s) official finding of guilty or not guilty. Not every legal decision is a verdict.

- **decision** — The judge’s decision to refuse bail surprised everyone in the crowded courtroom.
- **verdict** — After three days, the jury returned a guilty verdict on the robbery charge.

### commit / break

You **commit** a crime or an offence (commit murder, commit fraud). You **break** a law, a rule, or a promise. You do not usually say *commit a law*; you break it.

- **commit** — He admitted that he had committed fraud over a period of two years.
- **break** — Cyclists who break the new helmet law can be fined on the spot.

### rule / law / justice / right

A **rule** is a regulation, often of a school, club, or game. A **law** is an official rule of a country. **Justice** is fairness and the proper working of the legal system. A **right** is something you are legally or morally allowed to have or do.

- **rule** — Prison visitors must follow the rule about leaving phones at the entrance.
- **law** — There is a law against selling alcohol to anyone under eighteen.
- **justice** — Many people felt that justice had not been done after such a short sentence.
- **right** — Every accused person has the right to a lawyer and a fair hearing.

### judge / jury

A **judge** is the legal official who controls the trial and often decides the sentence. A **jury** is a group of ordinary people who listen to the evidence and decide if the accused is guilty. In many cases the judge does not decide guilt; the jury does.

- **judge** — The judge warned the witness not to guess, but to say only what she saw.
- **jury** — The jury spent a night discussing the fingerprints before they reached a verdict.

### prosecute / persecute

To **prosecute** is to take someone to court for a crime. To **persecute** is to treat someone cruelly, often over time, because of who they are (religion, politics, race). They sound similar but are not interchangeable.

- **prosecute** — The state decided to prosecute the company for dumping chemicals in the river.
- **persecute** — The journalist was persecuted for years after she published the corruption story.

### capital punishment / corporal punishment

**Capital punishment** is the death penalty. **Corporal punishment** is physical punishment, such as beating. Both are types of punishment; only capital punishment means execution.

- **capital punishment** — The country abolished capital punishment after a series of wrongful convictions.
- **corporal punishment** — Corporal punishment in schools is illegal, even if parents say they support it.

### robber / burglar / thief

A **thief** steals in a general sense. A **robber** steals from a person or place, often using threat or violence (a bank robber). A **burglar** breaks into a building to steal. All burglars are thieves; not all thieves are burglars.

- **robber** — The robbers forced the cashier to open the safe and then ran to a waiting car.
- **burglar** — A burglar got in through the kitchen window while the family was on holiday.
- **thief** — The thief took her phone from the café table when she turned to pay.

### vandal / hooligan

A **vandal** deliberately damages property (windows, walls, trains). A **hooligan** is violent and noisy in public, often in a group, and is strongly associated with football crowds. Vandals attack things; hooligans often attack people or cause public disorder.

- **vandal** — Vandals smashed the bus shelters and sprayed paint across the station wall.
- **hooligan** — Football hooligans fought in the street after the match and several were arrested.

### sentence / imprison

To **sentence** someone is for a court to state the punishment (a fine, community service, prison). To **imprison** someone is to put them in prison. A sentence is not always imprisonment.

- **sentence** — The court sentenced her to six months in prison for repeated shoplifting.
- **imprison** — They imprisoned the protest leaders without a trial, which caused international criticism.

### innocent / guilty

**Innocent** means not responsible for the crime. **Guilty** means responsible. In court the accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty.

- **innocent** — New DNA tests showed that the man in prison was innocent of the murder.
- **guilty** — He looked guilty when the officer asked where he had been that night.

### witness / bystander

A **witness** sees a crime or accident and can describe it, often in court. A **bystander** is simply someone who happens to be there; they may not have noticed anything useful. A bystander can become a witness if they saw what happened.

- **witness** — The only witness said the driver did not stop after hitting the motorbike.
- **bystander** — Several bystanders filmed the arrest, but none of them called the police.

### lawyer / solicitor

**Lawyer** is the general word for a legal professional. In England and Wales a **solicitor** is a lawyer who usually gives advice and prepares cases; barristers often speak in higher courts. In everyday English, people still say *lawyer* for both.

- **lawyer** — You should not sign that statement until your lawyer has read every line.
- **solicitor** — She called a solicitor as soon as the police asked her to come to the station.

## Phrasal verbs

- **back down** — The minister refused to back down, even after thousands signed the petition.
- **break out** — Two prisoners broke out through a tunnel and were missing for three days.
- **bring in** — Parliament plans to bring in stricter laws on knife crime next year.
- **chase after** — The officer chased after the motorbike until it disappeared into the market.
- **come forward** — Police asked witnesses to come forward with any information about the attack.
- **get away with** — He thought he could get away with lying in court, but the jury noticed.
- **go off** (explode) — The device went off in an empty street, so luckily nobody was injured.
- **go off** (be fired) — The officer’s gun went off by accident during the search of the flat.
- **hand in** — She handed in the fake passport at the desk and waited to be interviewed.
- **hold up** (rob) — Two men held up the petrol station and escaped with the night’s takings.
- **hold up** (delay) — The trial was held up for a week because a key witness was ill.
- **let off** (little punishment) — The magistrate let him off with a warning because it was a first offence.
- **let off** (explode) — Someone let off a firework outside the courthouse, and people ran inside.
- **look into** — Detectives are looking into how the confidential files left the police station.
- **make off** — The thieves made off with the painting before the alarm had even started.
- **take down** — The clerk took down everything the witness said, including the exact times.
- **take in** — The email took him in; he transferred the money before he realised it was fraud.

## Phrases and collocations

### account

- **on account of** — The hearing was delayed on account of the missing forensic report.
- **take into account** — The judge took her age into account when he decided the sentence.
- **account for sth** — Can you account for the cash that disappeared from the evidence room?

### advantage

- **take advantage of sth** — Burglars took advantage of the empty street during the football match.
- **take advantage of sb** — The gang took advantage of elderly people who lived alone.
- **have an advantage over sth/sb** — The prosecution had an advantage over the defence because of the CCTV.
- **at an advantage** — With a good lawyer, she felt at an advantage before the interview began.
- **an/one/etc advantage (of sth)** — One advantage of the new cameras is that fewer shops are robbed.

### blame

- **be to blame (for sth)** — The broken lock was to blame for the easy break-in last night.
- **be to blame (for doing)** — He is to blame for leaving the safe open while he went out.
- **get the blame (for sth/doing)** — The junior officer got the blame for a mistake the whole team made.
- **take the blame (for sth/doing)** — She took the blame for the lost files so that her colleague kept the job.
- **put the blame on sth/sb** — They put the blame on a neighbour, although they had no real evidence.
- **blame sth (on sb)** — The company blamed the leak on a former employee who had already left.
- **blame sb for sth** — Do not blame the jury for a verdict that followed the evidence they heard.
- **blame sb for doing** — Residents blamed the council for ignoring the vandalism for so long.

### damage

- **do damage (to sth)** — The explosion did serious damage to the windows along the high street.
- **cause damage (to sth)** — The flood caused damage to the courthouse basement and several files.

### fault

- **at fault** — The inquiry found that the security firm was at fault, not the night staff.
- **find fault with sth/sb** — The defence kept finding fault with every detail of the police statement.

### intention

- **have the intention of doing** — He had the intention of returning the wallet, but then he spent the cash.
- **have no intention of doing** — She had no intention of paying the fine until they sent a final warning.

### mistake

- **make a mistake** — The witness made a mistake about the colour of the getaway car.
- **a mistake (to do)** — It was a mistake to discuss the case in a public café near the court.
- **mistake sb for sb** — The officer mistook an innocent bystander for the man they wanted to arrest.
- **do sth by mistake** — He took the wrong briefcase by mistake and was stopped at security.

### necessary

- **necessary to do** — It was necessary to close the road while officers searched for the weapon.
- **necessary for sb to do** — It was necessary for her to identify the suspect from behind a screen.

### order

- **in order** — The documents were not in order, so the clerk refused to accept the application.
- **put sth in order** — Put these witness statements in order before the lawyer arrives at ten.
- **in order to do** — They searched the flat in order to find the stolen jewellery.
- **give an order (to sb) (to do)** — The sergeant gave an order to seal the street until the bomb team arrived.

### permission

- **give sb permission to do** — The judge gave them permission to film the first day of the trial.
- **ask (sb) for permission to do** — You must ask the court for permission to leave the country before the hearing.
- **have permission (from sb) to do** — He did not have permission from his solicitor to speak to journalists.
- **ask for permission (from sb) to do** — The reporters asked for permission to sit in the public gallery.
- **get permission (from sb) to do** — We got permission from the police to collect our belongings from the flat.

### purpose

- **do sth on purpose** — The vandals broke the cameras on purpose so nobody would film them.
- **purpose of sth** — The purpose of the new law is to stop shops selling knives to teenagers.

### reason

- **reason why** — The reason why she came forward was that she recognised the man from the news.
- **reason for sth** — There was no good reason for keeping him in the cell overnight.
- **reason with sb** — Officers tried to reason with the crowd, but bottles were already being thrown.

### solution

- **have a solution (to sth)** — The council still has no solution to the nightly vandalism in the park.
- **find a solution (to sth)** — Lawyers are trying to find a solution to the dispute without another trial.
- **think of a solution (to sth)** — Nobody could think of a solution that both families would accept.
- **work out a solution (to sth)** — They worked out a solution that avoided sending the teenager to prison.
- **come up with a solution (to sth)** — The detective came up with a solution after looking at the old files again.
- **figure out a solution (to sth)** — We need to figure out a solution before the next hearing on Friday.

### wrong

- **do wrong** — He knew he had done wrong as soon as he hid the phone in his bag.
- **do the wrong thing** — They did the wrong thing when they arrested a bystander instead of the thief.
- **the wrong thing to do** — Lying to the police is the wrong thing to do, even if you are frightened.
- **go wrong** — The raid went wrong when the alarm sounded earlier than they expected.
- **the wrong way up** — The exhibit photo was the wrong way up, so the jury asked for it to be turned.

## Word patterns

### accuse

- **accuse sb of sth** — They accused him of fraud after the company accounts were checked.
- **accuse sb of doing** — Neighbours accused her of hiding stolen goods in the garage.

### arrest

- **arrest sb for sth** — Police arrested the teenager for possession of a knife on the bus.
- **arrest sb for doing** — They arrested him for driving through the city while banned.

### charge

- **charge sb with sth** — Prosecutors charged the driver with causing death by dangerous driving.

### claim

- **claim to be** — He claimed to be a police officer so that the shop would open the till.
- **claim to do** — She claimed to know nothing about the money found in her locker.
- **claim that** — The accused claimed that he had been at home with his sister all evening.

### deny

- **deny sth** — The minister denied any connection with the illegal campaign funds.
- **deny doing** — She denied taking the documents, even when they showed her on camera.

### doubt

- **doubt sth** — I doubt the official story about how the prisoner managed to break out.
- **doubt that** — Few jurors doubted that the signature on the cheque was a forgery.
- **doubt if/whether** — I doubt whether the witness really saw the number plate in the dark.

### forgive

- **forgive sb for sth** — The family said they could not forgive him for the damage to their shop.
- **forgive sb for doing** — It is hard to forgive a friend for lying to the police about you.

### glimpse

- **glimpse sth** — She glimpsed a figure running across the car park just after the alarm.
- **catch a glimpse of sth** — He caught a glimpse of the getaway van before it turned the corner.

### guilty

- **guilty of sth** — The jury found her guilty of theft but not of the more serious charge.
- **guilty of doing** — He was found guilty of stealing the motorbike from outside the station.

### legal

- **legal to do** — It is not legal to carry a knife in public without a good reason.
- **legal for sb to do** — It is not legal for shops to sell alcohol to anyone under eighteen.

### make

- **make sb do** — The robbers made the manager open the safe while they watched the door.
- **be made to do** — The witness was made to wait in a side room until the jury had gone.

### refuse

- **refuse to do sth** — The suspect refused to answer any questions without a solicitor present.
- **refuse sth** — The court refused his request for bail because he had failed to appear before.

### respect

- **respect sth** — You have to respect the court’s decision, even if you think the sentence is light.
- **respect sb for sth** — People respected the officer for her calm work during the long investigation.
- **respect sb for doing** — I respect him for coming forward, although it put his job at risk.
- **have respect for sth** — She has little respect for a system that lets violent offenders walk free.
- **have respect for sb** — The jury had respect for the judge, who explained every step clearly.

### threaten

- **threaten to do** — The caller threatened to burn the warehouse unless they paid by Friday.
- **threaten sb with sth** — He threatened the cashier with a knife and demanded the cash drawer.
