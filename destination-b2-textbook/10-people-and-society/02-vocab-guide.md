# Unit 10 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### relationship / connection

A **relationship** is the way two people or groups feel and behave towards each other. A **connection** is a link, which can be personal, but is often more general (a family connection, a connection between two ideas).

- **relationship** — Their relationship has been difficult since they started working in the same office.
- **connection** — She got the job through a family connection, not through the public advertisement.

### blame / fault

**Blame** is usually a verb: you blame someone for something. **Fault** is a noun: it is your fault if you caused the problem. You can also find fault with someone.

- **blame** — Do not blame the children; the adults should have explained the rules more clearly.
- **fault** — It was not her fault that the train was late and she missed the interview.

### old / ancient

**Old** means having existed or lived for a long time (an old friend, an old building). **Ancient** is for the distant past, especially history (ancient Rome), not for a person you know.

- **old** — My old neighbour still knocks on the door every Sunday with homemade bread.
- **ancient** — The museum has a small collection of jewellery from ancient Greece and Egypt.

### crowd / audience

A **crowd** is a large group of people in a public place. An **audience** is the people watching a performance, film or talk.

- **crowd** — A crowd gathered in the square to hear the results of the local election.
- **audience** — The audience clapped for a long time after the young pianist had finished.

### enjoy / please

You **enjoy** something yourself (enjoy a party). You **please** someone else (try to please your parents). *Enjoy* is not usually used in the pattern *enjoy someone*.

- **enjoy** — I always enjoy family dinners, even when the conversation becomes a bit loud.
- **please** — He changed his plans just to please his grandmother, who hates travelling far.

### support / assist

**Support** means to help by agreeing, encouraging, or giving money or care. **Assist** is more formal and practical: to help someone do a task.

- **support** — Her friends supported her when she decided to leave the unpaid internship.
- **assist** — A volunteer assisted the elderly man with his shopping bags at the bus stop.

### kind / polite

**Kind** is about being generous and caring. **Polite** is about good manners, even if you do not feel warm towards the person.

- **kind** — It was kind of you to sit with her until her parents arrived at the hospital.
- **polite** — He was perfectly polite to the guests, but you could see he wanted to leave.

### sympathetic / likeable

**Sympathetic** means you understand someone’s problems and show that you care. **Likeable** means easy to like; a likeable person is pleasant, not necessarily sympathetic.

- **sympathetic** — The teacher was sympathetic when I explained why the homework was late.
- **likeable** — He is a likeable colleague, always ready with a joke in the kitchen at lunch.

### nervous / bad-tempered

**Nervous** means worried or tense, often before something happens. **Bad-tempered** means often angry or impatient, as a character trait or a mood.

- **nervous** — She gets nervous before family gatherings because she hates arguments at the table.
- **bad-tempered** — He is bad-tempered in the morning, so nobody speaks to him before coffee.

### sensitive / sensible

**Sensitive** means easily hurt, or aware of other people’s feelings. **Sensible** means showing good judgement; practical and not silly.

- **sensitive** — Be careful what you say; he is sensitive about not having gone to university.
- **sensible** — It was sensible to call her parents instead of walking home alone after midnight.

### company / group

**Company** can mean people you spend time with (*in someone’s company*) or a business. A **group** is a number of people together, often with a shared activity or identity.

- **company** — I enjoy her company, even when we just sit on the bench and say very little.
- **group** — A small group of neighbours started a weekly meal for anyone who lives alone.

### popular / famous

**Popular** means liked by many people. **Famous** means known by many people; you can be famous without being popular.

- **popular** — The new mayor is popular with young families because of the playgrounds she opened.
- **famous** — She became famous after the documentary, but she still lives in the same street.

### typical / usual / ordinary

**Typical** means showing the usual qualities of a type of person or thing. **Usual** means the same as what normally happens. **Ordinary** means not special or unusual.

- **typical** — It is typical of him to arrive last and then apologise with a big smile.
- **usual** — We met at the usual café, at the table by the window that nobody else uses.
- **ordinary** — They live an ordinary life: school runs, shopping, and dinner at half past six.

### close / near

**Close** often describes a relationship (*a close friend*) or a short distance in a more emotional or exact sense. **Near** is mainly about physical distance.

- **close** — She is still close to her sister, even though they live in different countries now.
- **near** — They bought a flat near the school so the children could walk there every morning.

### unknown / infamous

**Unknown** means not known or not famous. **Infamous** means famous for something bad.

- **unknown** — The author was unknown until a small local bookshop started recommending her novel.
- **infamous** — The street became infamous after the scandal, and many families moved away.

## Phrasal verbs

- **ask after** — She asked after your parents and was glad to hear they are both well.
- **bring up** — He was brought up by his aunt after his mother went abroad for work.
- **fall for** — She fell for him during the summer they both worked at the same café.
- **fall for** — He fell for the story about a lost wallet and handed over fifty pounds.
- **fall out (with)** — I fell out with my best friend over something that now seems completely silly.
- **get on (with)** — Do you get on with your new colleagues, or is the office still a bit cold?
- **grow up** — She grew up on a farm and still finds the city noise difficult at night.
- **look down on** — Some people look down on anyone who did not go to a famous university.
- **look up to** — The younger children look up to her because she never laughs at their mistakes.
- **make up** — They made up the same evening and went out for pizza as if nothing had happened.
- **pass away** — His uncle passed away in March, and the family is still sorting out the house.
- **pick on** — The other children picked on him because he spoke with a different accent.
- **put down** — She puts people down in meetings, then pretends it was only a joke.
- **settle down** — Settle down and tell us, without shouting, what the neighbour actually said.
- **settle down** — They settled down in York after the wedding and hardly travel any more.
- **stand up for** — You have to stand up for yourself, or people will keep walking over you.
- **take aback** — I was taken aback by her honesty; nobody in the family usually says that.

## Phrases and collocations

### approval

- **show (your) approval of/for sth** — The head teacher showed her approval of the plan with a short nod.
- **give (your) approval of/for sth** — The committee gave its approval for the community garden last night.
- **meet with sb's approval** — I hope this seating plan will meet with your grandmother’s approval.

### argument

- **have an argument (with sb)** — I had an argument with my brother about who should call our parents.
- **have an argument about sth/doing** — They had an argument about sharing the bill after a very long dinner.
- **win an argument** — She won the argument by staying calm and repeating the facts slowly.
- **lose an argument** — He lost the argument and then refused to speak for the rest of the evening.

### care

- **take care (of sth)** — Could you take care of the plants while we are away for two weeks?
- **take care (of sb)** — She took care of her younger brothers after school every day for years.
- **care for sth/sb** — He cares for his elderly father and still works full time in the town.
- **care about sth/sb** — She really cares about fairness, not just about looking generous in public.

### courage

- **have the courage to do** — I did not have the courage to tell her the truth at the party.
- **it takes courage to do** — It takes courage to apologise first after a public family argument.

### disguise

- **in disguise** — The actor appeared in disguise so that nobody in the café would recognise him.
- **wear a disguise** — They wore a disguise for the play, including a wig and a false beard.
- **disguise yourself** — She disguised herself as a tourist so she could watch the ceremony unnoticed.
- **disguised as sth/sb** — He arrived disguised as a waiter and surprised everyone at the table.

### dream

- **have a dream (about sth)** — I had a dream about our old house and woke up feeling strangely sad.
- **have a dream (about sb)** — She had a dream about her grandmother, who died more than ten years ago.
- **have a dream (about doing)** — He had a dream about speaking in front of a huge crowd of strangers.
- **daydream** — Stop daydreaming and listen; this is about your own exam timetable.
- **dream of/about doing** — She dreams of living by the sea with no neighbours for a mile.

### family

- **have a family** — They want to have a family, but they are waiting until they can afford a flat.
- **start a family** — We started a family later than our friends, and we do not regret the wait.
- **nuclear family** — In a nuclear family you usually have parents and their children under one roof.
- **extended family** — The wedding included the extended family, from great-aunts to tiny cousins.

### favour

- **do sb a favour** — Could you do me a favour and collect the children from school today?
- **owe sb a favour** — I owe her a favour after she looked after the dog for a whole month.
- **be in favour of** — Most of the street is in favour of a slower speed limit outside the school.

### friend

- **make friends (with sb)** — She made friends with a neighbour in the first week after she moved in.
- **become friends (with sb)** — They became friends slowly, after months of nodding on the same train.
- **be friends (with sb)** — I have been friends with him since we were both six years old.
- **stay friends (with sb)** — It is hard to stay friends with someone who never replies to messages.
- **best friend** — My best friend is the only person I would call in the middle of the night.

### love

- **be in love with sb** — They have been in love with each other since they were at college.
- **fall in love with sb** — She fell in love with him on a rainy bus ride across the city.

### mood

- **in a good mood** — Dad is in a good mood today, so it is a sensible time to ask about the trip.
- **in a bad mood** — Do not talk to her yet; she is in a bad mood after that phone call.
- **in the right/wrong mood** — I was in the wrong mood for a party and left after twenty minutes.
- **in the mood for sth** — Are you in the mood for a walk, or would you rather stay in?

### pity

- **pity sb** — I pity anyone who has to live with that kind of daily criticism at home.
- **take pity on sb** — A neighbour took pity on us and lent us a heater during the cold week.
- **feel pity for sb** — I felt pity for the boy sitting alone, but I did not know what to say.
- **it's a pity (that)** — It is a pity that they never made up after such a small disagreement.

### promise

- **promise to do** — She promised to call as soon as the plane landed, and she actually did.
- **give sb a promise** — He gave her a promise that he would never mention the argument again.
- **make sb a promise** — Do not make the children a promise unless you are sure you can keep it.
- **break a/your promise** — She broke her promise about keeping the secret, and now the whole class knows.

## Word patterns

### agree

- **agree with sth** — I agree with the idea of a neighbourhood watch, but not with cameras everywhere.
- **agree on sth** — We finally agreed on a time that both families could manage on Saturday.
- **agree to sth** — They agreed to the divorce settlement after three long meetings with lawyers.
- **agree with sb** — I agree with you about the noise, but shouting at the neighbours will not help.
- **agree to do** — She agreed to look after the baby so that we could go to the hospital.
- **agree that** — Everyone agreed that the party should end before midnight because of the baby.

### allow

- **allow sb to do** — They will not allow us to visit until the tests have all come back clear.
- **allow sth** — The building does not allow pets, which is why they had to find another flat.

### approve

- **approve of sth** — Her parents do not approve of her plan to travel alone for a year.
- **approve of doing** — I do not approve of discussing private family matters in front of guests.
- **approve sth** — The council approved the plan for a new community centre on the old car park.

### ask

- **ask sb sth** — I asked him his name twice, and I still could not hear it in the noise.
- **ask sb to do sth (for you)** — Could you ask Sarah to save us two seats near the back?
- **ask about sth** — She asked about the job, but she did not mention the salary at all.
- **ask for sth** — He asked for help, which for him is already a very big step.
- **ask if/whether** — Ask whether they need anything from the shop before you leave the house.

### attack

- **attack sth** — The article attacked the government’s plan without offering any better idea.
- **attack sb for sth/doing** — They attacked her for leaving the meeting early, although she was ill.
- **an attack on sth/sb** — The speech was a clear attack on people who live on benefits.

### ban

- **ban sb from sth/doing** — The club banned him from the hall after he started another fight.
- **ban sth** — Several schools have banned phones during lessons, and the students are furious.

### convince

- **convince sb (of sth)** — I could not convince him of the need to apologise first.
- **convince sb to do** — She convinced her father to come to the wedding after months of silence.
- **convince sb that** — He convinced us that moving in together was the right decision.

### force

- **force sb to do sth** — Nobody can force you to stay in a job that makes you unhappy.
- **force sb into sth/doing** — They forced her into accepting a role she had already refused twice.

### independent

- **independent of/from sth** — She wanted to be independent of her parents before she turned twenty-five.

### let

- **let sb do sth** — Please let him speak before you decide he is in the wrong.

### object

- **object to sth** — Several neighbours objected to the noisy party on a Sunday night.
- **object to doing** — I object to being treated like a child in my own house.

### pretend

- **pretend to be** — He pretended to be asleep so that nobody would ask him about the argument.
- **pretend to do** — She pretended to read the menu while she listened to the next table.
- **pretend that** — They pretended that everything was fine, which only made the children more anxious.

### rely

- **rely on sth** — You cannot rely on luck; you have to tell her how you actually feel.
- **rely on sb** — I know I can rely on you if the baby-sitter cancels at the last minute.
