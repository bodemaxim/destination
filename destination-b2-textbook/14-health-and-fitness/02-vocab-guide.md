# Unit 14 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### prescription / recipe

A **prescription** is a doctor’s written order for medicine. A **recipe** is instructions for cooking food. Do not use *recipe* for medicine in this sense.

- **prescription** — The doctor wrote a prescription for antibiotics and told me to rest at home.
- **recipe** — This recipe for vegetable soup is simple enough to cook when you feel unwell.

### operation / surgery

An **operation** is a specific surgical procedure (a heart operation). **Surgery** is the medical work of operating, or the place/specialism; you can have surgery or go into surgery. In everyday English they often overlap.

- **operation** — She had an operation on her knee and started physiotherapy the following week.
- **surgery** — He needs surgery on his back before he can return to heavy lifting at work.

### sore / hurt / pain

**Sore** is usually an adjective: a sore throat, sore muscles. **Hurt** is often a verb (my arm hurts) or an adjective after a verb (I got hurt). **Pain** is a noun: you feel pain, or have a pain in your chest.

- **sore** — My legs were sore after the first long run I had done in months.
- **hurt** — He hurt his shoulder during training and could not lift the bag afterwards.
- **pain** — She felt a sharp pain in her chest and went straight to the hospital.

### illness / disease

An **illness** is a period of being unwell; it can be short or long. A **disease** is a specific medical condition, often with a name (heart disease, infectious disease). You catch an illness; doctors treat a disease.

- **illness** — After a long illness she went back to work only two mornings a week.
- **disease** — The clinic specialises in treating people with heart disease and high blood pressure.

### injured / damaged

People and animals are **injured**. Things are **damaged**. You can also damage your health, but we do not usually say a *damaged person* after an accident.

- **injured** — Two injured cyclists were taken to hospital after the crash on the bridge.
- **damaged** — The fall damaged the ligaments in his ankle, so he needed a support bandage.

### thin / slim

**Thin** describes little body fat and can sound negative or worrying. **Slim** is more positive: attractively or healthily thin. A patient who has lost weight through illness is thin, not slim.

- **thin** — He looked thin after the operation and the nurse asked if he was eating enough.
- **slim** — She stayed slim by walking to work and cooking most of her meals at home.

### remedy / cure / therapy

A **remedy** is a treatment, often traditional or for a minor problem (a home remedy). A **cure** removes the illness completely. **Therapy** is a course of treatment (physio, speech therapy), not always a complete cure.

- **remedy** — Honey and lemon is an old remedy for a sore throat, but it is not a medicine.
- **cure** — There is still no cure for the disease, although treatment can slow it down.
- **therapy** — After the accident she had therapy twice a week to get her arm moving again.

### effect / result

An **effect** is what something causes (the effect of a drug, side effects). A **result** is the outcome of an action, test, or process (the result of a blood test). Tests have results; medicines have effects.

- **effect** — The medicine had a strong effect, and she felt sleepy within half an hour.
- **result** — The blood test result showed that he was low in iron and needed tablets.

### healthy / fit

**Healthy** means in good health generally, or good for your health (healthy food). **Fit** means strong and able to do physical activity. You can be healthy but not fit, or fit but eat badly.

- **healthy** — A healthy diet with less sugar helped her blood pressure fall over the summer.
- **fit** — He is fit enough to run a half marathon, although he still catches colds easily.

### examine / investigate

A doctor **examines** a patient: looks at the body. Police or researchers **investigate** a problem or crime. Hospitals may investigate an outbreak, but they examine people.

- **examine** — The doctor examined her throat and then sent her for a blood test.
- **investigate** — Scientists are investigating why the infection spread so quickly in the hospital.

### infection / pollution

An **infection** is caused by bacteria, viruses, or other germs in the body. **Pollution** is dirty air, water, or land in the environment. Pollution can make infections and breathing problems worse, but it is not an infection itself.

- **infection** — The wound developed an infection, so the nurse changed the dressing every day.
- **pollution** — Air pollution in the city makes her asthma worse on hot, still afternoons.

### plaster / bandage

A **plaster** (BrE) is a small sticky covering for a cut; **plaster** can also mean a plaster cast on a broken bone. A **bandage** is a strip of cloth wrapped around a larger injury.

- **plaster** — Put a plaster on that cut before you go back into the swimming pool.
- **bandage** — The nurse wrapped a bandage around his ankle and told him to keep it raised.

### ward / clinic

A **ward** is a room or area in a hospital where patients stay. A **clinic** is a place you visit for treatment or advice, often without staying overnight.

- **ward** — She spent three nights on the children’s ward after the asthma attack.
- **clinic** — I have an appointment at the clinic on Tuesday for a follow-up blood test.

### dose / fix

A **dose** is a measured amount of medicine. A **fix** is informal for a dose of an illegal drug that someone is addicted to. In medical English, use dose.

- **dose** — Do not take a second dose if you still have a headache after one tablet.
- **fix** — He was desperate for a fix, which is why he stole money from the house.

### fever / rash

A **fever** is a high body temperature. A **rash** is red or irritated skin. Some illnesses cause both.

- **fever** — The child had a high fever, so we called the doctor in the middle of the night.
- **rash** — A rash appeared on her arms after she started the new course of antibiotics.

## Phrasal verbs

- **break out** — Fighting broke out in the waiting room when two families started shouting.
- **bring on** — The cold air brought on a coughing fit, and she had to sit down at once.
- **come down with** — Half the team came down with a cold the week before the final match.
- **come round** — She came round in the ambulance and asked where her bike had gone.
- **come to** — He came to a few minutes after he passed out in the crowded gym.
- **cut down** — The doctor told him to cut down smoking if he wanted his lungs to recover.
- **cut down on** — I have cut down on sugar, and my energy in the afternoon is much better.
- **feel up to** — I do not feel up to training tonight; my throat still hurts when I swallow.
- **get over** — It took her a month to get over the flu and return to her usual runs.
- **give up** — He gave up eating fast food after the nurse explained his cholesterol results.
- **look after** — Neighbours looked after the children while their mother was in hospital.
- **pass out** — She passed out in the heat after running without drinking any water.
- **pull through** — The surgeons said he was lucky to pull through after such a serious infection.
- **put down** — The vet had to put the old dog down because it was in constant pain.
- **put on** — He put on six kilos during winter, when he stopped cycling to the office.
- **wear off** — When the painkillers wore off, her knee started to hurt on the stairs again.

## Phrases and collocations

### alternative

- **alternative medicine** — She tried alternative medicine after the standard tablets made her feel sick.
- **alternative therapy** — The clinic also offers alternative therapy, including acupuncture for back pain.
- **find an alternative (to sth)** — We need to find an alternative to this drug if the side effects continue.

### appointment

- **make an appointment** — You should make an appointment with the dentist before the pain gets worse.
- **have an appointment** — I have an appointment at the health centre at three this afternoon.
- **break an appointment** — Do not break an appointment at the clinic unless you really cannot attend.

### bath

- **have a bath** — Have a bath in warm water if your muscles are sore after the long walk.
- **take a bath** — He took a bath to relax his back before going to physiotherapy.
- **run a bath (for sb)** — I ran a bath for her when she came home shaking with a fever.

### danger

- **in danger** — The climber was in danger of frostbite until the rescue team arrived.
- **out of danger** — After two days on the ward, the nurse said the baby was out of danger.

### exercise

- **do an exercise** — Do this exercise ten times slowly if you want to strengthen your knees.
- **do exercise** — She does exercise every morning, even when she only has fifteen minutes.
- **take (some) exercise** — You should take some exercise each day, even a short walk after lunch.
- **get (some) exercise** — He gets some exercise by cycling to the clinic instead of driving.

### fit

- **get fit** — She joined a swimming class to get fit before the trekking holiday in June.
- **stay fit** — He stays fit by running twice a week and walking to work on the other days.
- **keep fit** — They keep fit in winter by using the gym in the basement of the hospital.
- **be fit** — You need to be fit if you want to work as a mountain rescue volunteer.
- **fit and healthy** — The coach wants the whole squad to arrive fit and healthy in September.

### good

- **do sb good** — A weekend without screens would do you good after such a stressful month.
- **sth does you good** — Fresh air does you good, especially if you sit at a desk all day.
- **good for sb (to do)** — It is good for her to stretch before she starts the long physiotherapy session.

### health

- **in good health** — Grandfather is in good health for his age and still walks to the shops.
- **in bad health** — She has been in bad health since the operation and tires very quickly.
- **in poor health** — Patients in poor health should not skip the follow-up appointment next week.
- **health centre** — The health centre on the high street now opens on Saturday mornings as well.
- **health care** — Good health care in that region is still hard to reach for people in villages.

### injection

- **have an injection (for sth)** — He had an injection for tetanus after cutting his hand on rusty metal.
- **have an injection (against sth)** — Travellers should have an injection against hepatitis before they leave.
- **give sb an injection** — The nurse gave the child an injection and then put a plaster on the arm.

### medicine

- **take medicine** — Take the medicine after food, or it may upset your stomach in the morning.
- **prescribe medicine** — The doctor prescribed medicine for the infection and asked her to return on Friday.
- **practise medicine** — She has practised medicine in this town for twenty years and knows most families.
- **study medicine** — He wants to study medicine, so he volunteers at the hospital every summer.
- **the best medicine** — Sleep is often the best medicine when you have a simple cold.
- **alternative medicine** — Some patients combine alternative medicine with the treatment their GP recommends.

### shape

- **get in shape** — I need to get in shape before the charity run at the end of the month.
- **get into shape** — She got into shape by swimming lengths three times a week after work.
- **stay in shape** — He stays in shape during winter with short indoor sessions on the bike.
- **keep in shape** — Dancers keep in shape with daily classes, even when they are not performing.
- **the shape of sth** — The shape of the bruise on her leg matched the edge of the table.
- **in the shape of** — Help arrived in the shape of a paramedic who was already in the building.

### spread

- **spread sth** — Wash your hands so that you do not spread the infection through the office.
- **spread sth over sth** — Spread the cream over the rash twice a day until it starts to fade.
- **spread sth on sth** — Spread the ointment on the burn and then cover it with a light bandage.
- **spread to a place** — The flu spread to neighbouring towns within a week of the first cases.

## Word patterns

### addicted

- **addicted to sth** — He became addicted to painkillers after the operation and needed extra support.

### attempt

- **attempt to do** — She attempted to stand up too soon and felt dizzy beside the hospital bed.

### benefit

- **benefit from sth** — Older patients benefit from short daily walks more than from sitting still.
- **a benefit of sth** — One benefit of cycling to work is that you arrive already a little fitter.

### complain

- **complain about sth** — Several patients complained about the long wait at the blood-test clinic.
- **complain about sb doing** — He complained about the neighbours smoking in the corridor outside his ward.
- **complain to sb** — If the pain continues, complain to the nurse rather than waiting until morning.
- **complain of sth** — She complained of a headache and a stiff neck after the car accident.

### cope

- **cope with sth** — He is learning to cope with the diet the diabetes nurse gave him.
- **cope with doing** — She coped with working night shifts until the baby started sleeping longer.

### inject

- **inject sth into sth** — The nurse injected the vaccine into his upper arm and asked him to wait.
- **inject sth into sb** — Doctors injected antibiotics into the patient as soon as she reached the ward.

### lead

- **lead to sth** — Too little sleep can lead to poor concentration and more mistakes at work.
- **lead to (your) doing** — Ignoring the pain led to his injuring the same knee again in training.

### likely

- **likely to do** — You are likely to feel tired for a few days after this type of surgery.
- **it is likely that** — It is likely that the rash will fade once you stop the new cream.
- **it is unlikely that** — It is unlikely that she will be fit enough to race this weekend.

### need

- **need to do** — You need to finish the course of tablets even if you already feel better.
- **need doing** — These bandages need changing every morning until the wound stays clean and dry.
- **in need of** — The small clinic is in need of more nurses for the evening asthma sessions.
- **no need for** — There is no need for another X-ray if the swelling continues to go down.

### operate

- **operate on sb** — The surgeon operated on her for six hours after the cycling accident.
- **operate on sth** — They operated on his heart last spring, and he has walked further every month.

### suffer

- **suffer from sth** — Many office workers suffer from back pain because they sit still for too long.
- **suffer sth** — He suffered a stroke at fifty and had to relearn how to walk and speak.

### tired

- **tired of sth** — I am tired of this cough; it has lasted since the beginning of winter.
- **tired of doing** — She is tired of waiting for a physiotherapy appointment that never seems to arrive.

### try

- **try to do** — Try to drink more water when you have a fever and cannot eat a full meal.
- **try sth** — The physiotherapist asked him to try this stretch before he goes running again.
- **try sb** — If this GP cannot help, you can try someone at the sports injury clinic.
- **try doing** — Try sleeping with an extra pillow if your breathing is worse at night.
- **try and do** — Try and rest your ankle today, even if you feel impatient to walk on it.

### worry

- **worry about sth** — Do not worry about a mild fever for one evening if she is still drinking.
- **worry about sb doing** — His parents worry about him training so hard after the recent concussion.
- **worried that** — She was worried that the lump might be serious, so she booked a scan quickly.
- **worried about** — He is worried about the side effects of the new medicine on his stomach.
- **worried by** — The doctor was worried by how quickly the infection had spread down the leg.

### worth

- **worth sth** — The extra physiotherapy sessions were worth the cost, because her shoulder finally improved.
- **worth doing** — It is worth doing a warm-up, even if you only have ten minutes to train.
