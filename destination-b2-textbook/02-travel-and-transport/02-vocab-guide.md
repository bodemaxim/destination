# Unit 2 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### voyage / journey / trip / travel / excursion

A **voyage** is a long journey by sea or in space. A **journey** is the process of going from one place to another, often over a long distance, by any means of transport. A **trip** is usually shorter and often means going somewhere and coming back, often for a particular purpose. **Travel** is uncountable and names the general activity of going from place to place. An **excursion** is a short organised outing, usually for pleasure.

- **voyage** — After three weeks at sea, the long voyage across the Atlantic finally came to an end.
- **journey** — The train journey from Edinburgh to London took just over four and a half hours.
- **trip** — We are planning a weekend trip to Brighton to visit some old university friends.
- **travel** — She has always loved travel and spends several months abroad every single year.
- **excursion** — The hotel organised a short excursion to a nearby castle for all the guests.

### fare / ticket / fee

A **fare** is the money you pay for a journey on a bus, train, taxi, or similar. A **ticket** is the paper or electronic document that allows you to travel or to enter. A **fee** is money paid for a professional service, or for permission to do something.

- **fare** — The bus fare into the city centre has gone up twice this year already.
- **ticket** — I bought a return ticket online so I would not have to queue at the station.
- **fee** — You must pay a small fee if you want to bring extra luggage on this flight.

### live / stay

**Live** means to have your home in a place, usually on a permanent or long-term basis. **Stay** means to remain somewhere for a limited time, for example in a hotel or with friends.

- **live** — My sister lives in Manchester, but she comes down to London most weekends.
- **stay** — We decided to stay at a small guesthouse near the harbour for three nights.

### view / sight

A **view** is the scenery you can see from a particular place, such as a window or a hill. A **sight** is something worth seeing, or the ability to see; visitors go out to see the sights of a city.

- **view** — From our hotel balcony we had a spectacular view of the mountains at sunrise.
- **sight** — The old harbour at dusk is a beautiful sight that visitors never forget.

### miss / lose

**Miss** means to fail to catch, attend, or reach something, or to feel sad because a person or place is not there. **Lose** means to no longer have something, or to be unable to find it.

- **miss** — If we do not leave now, we will almost certainly miss the last train home.
- **lose** — She lost her luggage at the airport and had to wait two days for it.

### border / edge / line

A **border** is the official dividing line between two countries. An **edge** is the outer limit of a physical thing, such as a road, a cliff, or a table. A **line** can be a long thin mark, a row of people or vehicles, or a transport route.

- **border** — We showed our passports and crossed the border into France just after midday.
- **edge** — The path ran along the edge of the cliff, with the sea far below us.
- **line** — There was a long line of cars waiting to board the ferry to the island.

### world / earth

**World** usually means the planet as a place of people, societies, and experience, or a particular field of activity. **Earth** is the planet as a physical object, or the soil; we also say *on earth*.

- **world** — After a year of travel, she felt she had seen a completely different world.
- **earth** — From space, the earth looks like a small blue sphere surrounded by darkness.

### take / bring / go

**Take** means to carry something or someone to another place, usually away from where the speaker is now. **Bring** means to carry something towards the speaker or the listener. **Go** means to move yourself to a place; you do not take or bring yourself.

- **take** — Could you take these bags to the taxi while I check us out of the hotel?
- **bring** — Please bring your passport to reception tomorrow morning so we can copy it.
- **go** — We should go to the station an hour early if we want decent seats.

### length / distance

**Length** is how long something is from one end to the other. **Distance** is how far apart two points or places are.

- **length** — The length of the new tunnel under the river is just over three kilometres.
- **distance** — The distance between the airport and the city centre is almost forty kilometres.

### area / territory

An **area** is a region or part of a place, used in a fairly neutral way. **Territory** is land that belongs to or is controlled by a country, an army, or an animal.

- **area** — This area of the city is popular with tourists because of its old streets.
- **territory** — The soldiers were ordered not to cross into enemy territory under any circumstances.

### book / keep

**Book** means to reserve a seat, ticket, table, or room in advance. **Keep** means to continue to have something, or not to give it back.

- **book** — You should book your ferry tickets well in advance during the summer months.
- **keep** — You can keep the hire car until Monday, but please return it before noon.

### guide / lead

**Guide** means to show someone around a place and give them information. **Lead** means to go in front so that others follow, or to be in charge of a group.

- **guide** — She offered to guide us through the narrow streets to the hidden viewpoint.
- **lead** — The experienced climber will lead the group along the mountain path at dawn.

### season / period

A **season** is a time of year, or the part of the year when something typically happens, such as the tourist season. A **period** is any stretch of time; it is not tied to the calendar year.

- **season** — The tourist season on this island lasts from June until the end of September.
- **period** — After a long period of waiting, the delayed flight was finally ready for boarding.

### arrive / reach

**Arrive** means to get to a place. We say *arrive in* a city or country and *arrive at* a building, station, or event. **Reach** means to get as far as a place; it takes a direct object and needs no preposition.

- **arrive** — We arrived at the airport two hours early, but the check-in desks were closed.
- **reach** — After twelve hours on the road, we finally reached the village just before midnight.

### native / home

**Native** describes a person or thing that belongs to a place by birth. **Home** is the place where you live, or the town or country you belong to.

- **native** — He is a native of Crete and knows every hidden beach along the southern coast.
- **home** — After three months abroad, he was glad to be home and sleep in his own bed.

## Phrasal verbs

- **catch up with** — It took us an hour to catch up with the other cyclists on the mountain road.
- **check in** — We had to check in at the airport three hours before our flight to Tokyo.
- **check out** — We need to check out of the hotel by eleven if we want the last ferry.
- **check out** — The police want to check out a report of a stolen car near the station.
- **drop off** — Could you drop me off at the station on your way into town?
- **drop off** — I always drop off as soon as the coach leaves the city and hits the motorway.
- **get back** — We did not get back from the airport until well after midnight last night.
- **go away** — They usually go away to the south coast for two weeks every August.
- **keep up with** — It was hard to keep up with the tour group as they rushed through the museum.
- **make for** — After the storm, the fishing boats made for the nearest harbour as quickly as possible.
- **pick up** — I will pick you up from the station at six and drive you to the hotel.
- **pull in** — The driver pulled in at the side of the road to look at the map.
- **run over** — A careless driver nearly ran over a cyclist who was crossing the busy junction.
- **see off** — We went to the station to see her off before she left for university.
- **set out** — We set out at dawn so that we would reach the mountain hut before dark.
- **set off** — They set off for the airport far too late and nearly missed their flight.
- **take off** — The plane took off twenty minutes late because of thick fog over the runway.
- **turn round** — We had to turn round on the motorway when we realised we had missed the exit.

## Phrases and collocations

### accident

- **have an accident** — We had an accident on the motorway and had to wait an hour for the police.
- **be (involved) in an accident** — Two coaches were involved in an accident just outside the city this morning.
- **do sth by accident** — I booked the wrong hotel by accident and only noticed after I had paid.

### advance

- **in advance** — You should book your ferry tickets in advance if you are travelling in August.
- **advance to/towards a place** — The hikers slowly advanced towards the mountain hut as the weather began to worsen.

### ahead

- **go straight ahead** — Go straight ahead at the roundabout and the station will be on your right.
- **go ahead** — The airline said we could go ahead and board even though our bags were delayed.
- **be ahead of sth** — We were ahead of schedule, so we stopped at a café near the harbour.
- **be ahead of sb** — The first cyclist was well ahead of the others by the time they reached the hill.

### direction

- **a change of direction** — After a sudden change of direction, the coach left the motorway and headed for the coast.
- **in the direction of sth** — We walked in the direction of the old harbour until we found a cheap guesthouse.
- **in this/that direction** — The taxi driver pointed that way and said the station was in that direction.

### head

- **off the top of your head** — Off the top of my head, I would say the journey takes about four hours.
- **head for/towards a place** — After breakfast we packed the car and headed for the next town along the coast.
- **head over heels (in love)** — She fell head over heels in love with the city on her first weekend there.

### holiday

- **go on holiday** — They go on holiday to Greece every summer and always rent a small house by the sea.
- **be on holiday** — I am on holiday this week, so I will not be checking work emails at all.
- **have a holiday** — We have not had a proper holiday in years, so this trip really matters to us.
- **take a holiday** — She decided to take a holiday in October, when the beaches would be much quieter.
- **bank holiday** — The trains were packed on the bank holiday weekend because so many people were travelling.

### left

- **go left** — At the traffic lights go left and you will see the bus station immediately.
- **turn left** — Turn left after the bridge and the hotel car park will be on your right.
- **on the left** — The ticket office is on the left as you enter the main station hall.
- **on the left-hand side** — Please keep your luggage on the left-hand side of the aisle during the journey.
- **in the left-hand corner** — Our table was in the left-hand corner of the café, with a view of the square.
- **left-handed** — The tour guide was left-handed, so he pointed at the map with his left hand.

### route

- **plan your/a route** — We spent the evening planning our route through the mountains before we set off.
- **take a route** — The driver took a longer route to avoid the heavy traffic in the city centre.

### sights

- **see the sights** — We spent the whole morning seeing the sights before our train left in the afternoon.

### sightseeing

- **go sightseeing** — After we checked in, we went sightseeing and walked along the river until sunset.

### speed

- **at (high/full/etc) speed** — The ambulance went past us at high speed on its way to the accident.
- **a burst of speed** — With a sudden burst of speed, the cyclist overtook the rest of the group.
- **speed limit** — The speed limit on this country road is only forty miles per hour.

### tour

- **go on a tour** — Tomorrow we are going on a tour of the old town with a local guide.
- **take a tour** — We took a tour around the castle and learned a great deal about its history.
- **tour a place** — They toured the island by scooter and stopped at every village along the coast.
- **tour guide** — Our tour guide spoke excellent English and knew all the quiet streets away from the crowds.

### trip

- **business trip** — He is in Frankfurt this week on a business trip and flies home on Friday.
- **school trip** — The school trip to the museum was cancelled because the coach broke down overnight.
- **go on a trip** — We are going on a trip to Edinburgh next month to see some old friends.
- **take a trip (to a place)** — They took a trip to the lake at dawn, before the tourist boats arrived.

### way

- **lose your way** — We lost our way in the old town and had to ask a local for directions.
- **make your way** — After the concert we made our way slowly back to the hotel through the crowds.
- **find your way** — With the map on my phone I soon found my way to the hidden viewpoint.
- **in a way** — In a way, missing the first train was lucky, because we met at the café.
- **on the way** — We stopped for petrol on the way to the airport and nearly missed the flight.
- **go all the way (to sth/swh)** — This bus goes all the way to the airport, so you do not need to change.

## Word patterns

### afraid

- **afraid of sth** — She is afraid of storms, so she will not take the ferry if the sea looks rough.
- **afraid of sb** — The children were afraid of the strict ticket inspector and hid behind their parents.
- **afraid of doing** — He is afraid of missing the connection, so he always runs through the station.
- **afraid to do** — I was afraid to ask the driver to stop, even though I felt quite unwell.

### appear

- **appear to be** — The train appeared to be delayed, but nobody had made an announcement yet.

### arrange

- **arrange sth** — I will arrange the tickets this evening so we can leave first thing tomorrow.
- **arrange sth with sb** — We arranged a pickup with the hotel driver for six o'clock the next morning.
- **arrange for sb to do** — I arranged for our neighbour to water the plants while we were away on holiday.

### arrive

- **arrive in a place** — We arrived in Lisbon just after midnight and took a taxi straight to the hotel.
- **arrive at a place** — They arrived at the airport with plenty of time to spare before check-in opened.
- **arrive here** — If you arrive here before noon, wait in reception and I will collect you.
- **arrive there** — Call me when you arrive there so I know the overnight coach got in safely.

### continue

- **continue sth** — We continued our journey after a short break at a service station near Bristol.
- **continue doing** — They continued walking along the coastal path even after it started to rain heavily.
- **continue to do** — The ferry continued to rock as we crossed the channel in the strong wind.
- **continue with sth** — After the delay we continued with our original plan and still reached the hut by dusk.

### differ

- **differ from sth** — This timetable differs from the one online, so please check the board before you leave.
- **differ from sb** — I differ from my brother about the best way to get there without a car.

### dream

- **dream about sth** — I often dream about that little hotel by the lake when the winter days feel long.
- **dream of sb** — She still dreams of the kind tour guide who showed her around Rome years ago.
- **dream of doing** — He has always dreamed of travelling around South America with nothing but a backpack.

### invite

- **invite sb to do** — They invited us to join them on a day trip to the mountains next Saturday.

### keen

- **keen to do** — She is keen to leave early so that we avoid the worst of the traffic.
- **keen on sth** — He is keen on rail travel and prefers overnight trains to cheap flights whenever possible.
- **keen on sb** — The students were keen on their tour guide and asked him to join them for dinner.
- **keen on doing** — We are keen on hiring bikes so we can explore the coast at our own pace.

### live

- **live in a place** — They live in Edinburgh now, but they still spend every August on the west coast.
- **live at a place** — She lives at 14 Harbour Street, which is only a short walk from the ferry.
- **live on sth** — For a month we lived on bread and fruit because the island shops were so expensive.
- **live for sth** — He lives for travel and spends every spare pound on the next long-distance train ticket.
- **live here** — If you live here, you soon get used to the ferries running late in winter.
- **live there** — They used to live there before the airport expanded and the noise became unbearable.

### regret

- **regret doing** — I regret booking the overnight coach; I hardly slept and felt ill all morning.
- **regret not doing** — She regrets not buying a ticket in advance, because the fare is much higher now.
- **regret sth** — He still regrets that missed connection, which cost him a whole extra night in the station.
- **regret to tell/inform you** — We regret to inform you that this evening's ferry has been cancelled due to high winds.

### think

- **think of sth** — Can you think of a cheaper way to get there without changing trains twice?
- **think about sb** — I thought about you when the train passed through your home town this morning.
- **think of doing** — We are thinking of staying an extra night if the weather holds until Sunday.

### write

- **write about sth** — She wrote about the overnight train in her diary as soon as she reached the hotel.
- **write about sb** — He wrote about his grandfather, who had worked as a porter at the same station.
- **write about doing** — I wrote about getting lost in Venice and then finding the quiet square by accident.
- **write sth to sb** — Please write a short note to the landlord before we leave the flat on Sunday.
- **write sb sth** — I wrote her a postcard from the ferry and posted it as soon as we docked.
- **write sth down** — Write the platform number down, or you will forget it in the rush to board.
