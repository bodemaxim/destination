# Unit 4 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### pitch / track / court / course / ring / rink

These words name the place where a sport is played. A **pitch** is a grass or artificial field for football, rugby or hockey. A **track** is a loop for running or motor racing. A **court** is a marked rectangle for tennis, basketball or badminton. A **course** is a longer route, especially for golf or horse racing. A **ring** is the square for boxing or wrestling. A **rink** is a sheet of ice for skating or ice hockey.

- **pitch** — The football pitch was so wet after the storm that the referee called the match off.
- **track** — She ran ten laps of the athletics track before the rest of the club even arrived.
- **court** — We booked the tennis court for an hour and played until it started raining.
- **course** — The golf course beside the river is one of the most difficult in the country.
- **ring** — The two boxers touched gloves in the ring and waited for the first bell.
- **rink** — The children spent the whole afternoon on the ice rink, falling over and laughing.

### umpire / referee

An **umpire** makes decisions in sports such as tennis, cricket and baseball. A **referee** does the same job in football, rugby, boxing and many other sports. Use the word that belongs to that sport; they are not freely interchangeable.

- **umpire** — The tennis umpire overruled the line judge and gave the point to the server.
- **referee** — The referee blew the whistle for half time after a very bad foul in the box.

### sport / athletics

**Sport** is the general word for physical games and competitions. **Athletics** is the group of running, jumping and throwing events, often called track and field.

- **sport** — Football is still the most popular sport at our school, followed by basketball.
- **athletics** — She gave up football and took up athletics so she could train for the 800 metres.

### win / beat / score

**Win** takes the event or prize: you win a match, a race or a medal. **Beat** takes the opponent: you beat a team or a player. **Score** means to get a goal, a point or a run.

- **win** — If we win this match, we will go through to the final on Saturday.
- **beat** — They beat the champions 3–1 and the whole stadium stood up and cheered.
- **score** — He scored twice in the second half and saved the team from another draw.

### final / finale / end / ending

A **final** is the last match in a competition. A **finale** is the last, often spectacular, part of a show or concert. The **end** is the point when something stops. An **ending** is the way a story or film finishes.

- **final** — Thousands of fans travelled to London to watch the cup final at Wembley.
- **finale** — The show’s finale included fireworks and every dancer on the stage at once.
- **end** — At the end of the game both captains shook hands in the centre circle.
- **ending** — I hated the ending of that film about the Olympic runner who never won.

### interval / half time

An **interval** is a break in a play, concert or long performance. **Half time** is the break in the middle of a football, rugby or hockey match.

- **interval** — During the interval we bought ice cream and talked about the first act.
- **half time** — At half time the coach told the players to stay calm and keep passing.

### play / game

As a noun, a **play** is a piece of theatre; as a verb, **play** means to take part in a sport. A **game** is a match or an activity with rules, including board games and video games. In British English we say *play football* but *a game of tennis*.

- **play** — We are going to see a new play at the local theatre on Friday evening.
- **game** — After school they always play a quick game of basketball behind the gym.

### bat / stick / rod / racket

A **bat** is used in cricket and baseball. A **stick** is used in hockey. A **rod** is used for fishing. A **racket** (also spelled *racquet*) is used in tennis, squash and badminton.

- **bat** — He swung the cricket bat too early and missed the ball completely.
- **stick** — Her hockey stick broke in half after a very hard tackle near the goal.
- **rod** — He sat by the lake all morning with a fishing rod and caught nothing at all.
- **racket** — I need a new tennis racket because the strings on this one keep snapping.

### draw / equal

A **draw** is a result in which both sides have the same score; you can also **draw** a match. **Equal** means the same in number or quality; a game can be equal, or one team can equal a record.

- **draw** — The match ended in a draw, so both teams still have a chance of the title.
- **equal** — Her time equalled the school record, but she did not quite break it.

### spectator / viewer

A **spectator** watches an event in person, in a stadium or at the courtside. A **viewer** watches on television or online.

- **spectator** — Spectators at the tennis final were asked not to move during the rallies.
- **viewer** — Millions of viewers stayed up late to watch the Olympic final on television.

### amateur / professional

An **amateur** plays for enjoyment and is not paid. A **professional** plays as a job and earns money from the sport.

- **amateur** — He is still an amateur boxer, although several clubs have offered him contracts.
- **professional** — She turned professional at eighteen and now plays tennis tournaments all year.

### competitor / opponent

A **competitor** is anyone taking part in a competition. An **opponent** is the person or team you are playing against in a particular match.

- **competitor** — Every competitor had to show a passport before they could enter the stadium.
- **opponent** — Respect your opponent, even when the referee gives a decision you do not like.

## Phrasal verbs

- **bring forward** — They brought forward the kick-off so families could get home before dark.
- **carry on** — Carry on stretching until the coach tells you it is time to start the drills.
- **get round to** — I finally got round to oiling my bike after months of meaning to do it.
- **get up to** — What are you getting up to this weekend besides the usual football practice?
- **get up to** — Those boys were getting up to no good behind the sports hall after dark.
- **go in for** — She went in for the club championship even though she had barely trained.
- **go in for** — He has never really gone in for team sports; he prefers swimming alone.
- **go off** — I went off jogging after I hurt my knee on that icy path last winter.
- **join in** — A few shy players joined in the warm-up once they saw it was only a game.
- **knock out** — We were knocked out of the cup by a much stronger side in extra time.
- **knock out** — The punch knocked him out, and the doctor would not let him fight again.
- **look out** — Look out! That cricket ball is coming straight towards the open window.
- **pull out** — Two teams pulled out of the tournament after they could not pay the fee.
- **put off** — They put off the race until Friday because the track was covered in ice.
- **put up with** — I will not put up with people shouting at the referee for the whole match.
- **take to** — She took to climbing so quickly that the instructors moved her up a group.
- **take up** — He took up squash in March and already plays twice a week after work.
- **take up** — The new gym equipment takes up most of the space in the old dance studio.

## Phrases and collocations

### best

- **make the best of sth** — We made the best of the rain and practised passing under the sports hall roof.
- **do your best** — Just do your best in the trial and try not to worry about the other runners.
- **the best at sth** — She is the best at chess in our year and has already won two school cups.
- **the best at doing** — He is the best at keeping goal when the other team attacks down the wing.

### chance

- **have a chance to do** — You will have a chance to try the new climbing wall after the lesson.
- **take a chance to do** — Take a chance to speak to the coach before she leaves the club tonight.
- **get a chance to do** — I never get a chance to play because the older students take all the matches.
- **have a chance of doing** — We still have a chance of winning the league if the leaders drop points.
- **some/little chance of doing** — There is little chance of finishing the marathon if you start too fast.
- **the chances of doing** — The chances of beating them on their home pitch are really quite small.
- **take a chance (on sth)** — I took a chance on a cheap second-hand bike and it has been perfect.
- **chance of a lifetime** — Playing at Wembley was the chance of a lifetime for the amateur side.

### go

- **your go** — It is your go now, so throw the dice and move your piece along the board.
- **have a go** — Have a go at the penalty shoot-out, even if you have never tried it before.

### height

- **in height** — The new climbing wall is twelve metres in height and looks rather frightening.
- **afraid of heights** — She is afraid of heights, so she will not go near the top diving board.
- **height of sth** — At the height of his career he was playing tennis in Grand Slam finals.

### mad

- **mad about sth** — He is mad about cricket and watches every test match on television.
- **mad on sb** — The younger fans are mad on the new striker and buy every shirt with his name.
- **mad about doing** — She is mad about swimming in the sea, even when the water is freezing.
- **go mad** — Dad will go mad if we kick the football through the kitchen window again.
- **become mad** — The crowd became mad with excitement when the underdog scored in extra time.

### pleasure

- **take pleasure in sth** — He takes pleasure in every small improvement in his running times.
- **take pleasure in doing** — She takes pleasure in coaching the children’s Saturday morning class.
- **gain pleasure from sth** — I gain little pleasure from video games; I would rather be outdoors.
- **get pleasure from doing** — They get pleasure from cycling along the river after a long week at work.

### popular

- **popular with/among** — Yoga has become very popular with office workers who sit all day.

### side

- **(on) the opposite side** — Their fans stood on the opposite side of the stadium and never sat down.
- **(on) the far side** — The spare balls are kept on the far side of the court, near the fence.
- **side with sb** — I sided with the referee, even though my own team was shouting at him.
- **on the winning side** — It feels good to be on the winning side after three heavy defeats.
- **on the losing side** — Nobody likes finishing on the losing side, but they still shook hands.

### talent

- **have a talent for sth** — She has a real talent for gymnastics and already trains five days a week.
- **have a talent for doing** — He has a talent for reading the game and always knows where to pass.
- **talent contest** — She won the school talent contest with a dance she had written herself.

### time

- **on time** — Please be on time for training; the coach locks the changing rooms at six.
- **(just) in time** — We arrived just in time to see the last penalty of the shoot-out.
- **the whole time** — He talked the whole time and did not watch a single rally of the match.
- **high/about time** — It is about time this club invested in a decent set of tennis nets.
- **take your time (doing)** — Take your time lining up the putt; there is no need to rush it.
- **take time to do** — Take time to warm up properly or you will pull a muscle in the first sprint.
- **sth takes up (your) time** — Training four nights a week takes up a lot of my free time.
- **spend time doing** — We spent time practising free throws until everyone could score ten in a row.
- **spend time on** — Do not spend too much time on one puzzle if the others are still waiting.
- **at/for a certain time** — Training is at a certain time each Tuesday, so please do not arrive late.
- **time passes** — Time passes quickly when you are absorbed in a long game of chess.
- **find time to do** — I must find time to mend my bike before the Sunday morning ride.
- **make time for** — She always makes time for a short run before she starts work.
- **find time for** — He never finds time for stretching, and then he wonders why he gets injured.
- **for the time being** — For the time being we will train indoors until the pitch dries out.
- **have a good/nice time (doing)** — Did you have a good time watching the race with your cousins?
- **tell the time** — Young children in the club are still learning how to tell the time on the clock.
- **free/spare/leisure time** — In his spare time he restores old racing bikes in the garage.

### turn

- **turn (a)round** — Turn around and face the bowler before he starts his run-up.
- **turn away** — She turned away from the screen because she could not watch the last penalty.
- **turn sth over** — Turn the card over only when the quizmaster says that your time is up.
- **in turn** — Each runner in turn carried the torch for one kilometre through the town.
- **take turns** — The children took turns on the climbing wall so that everyone felt safe.
- **take it in turn(s) (to do)** — We took it in turns to keep goal while the others practised shooting.
- **your turn (to do)** — It is your turn to choose the music for the warm-up in the gym.

## Word patterns

### compete

- **compete against sb** — Next week we compete against the county champions on their own court.
- **compete with sb** — It is hard to compete with clubs that can pay their players a full salary.
- **compete for sth** — Eight teams will compete for the trophy in the final round on Sunday.
- **compete in sth** — She hopes to compete in the national athletics championships next summer.

### concentrate

- **concentrate on sth** — You must concentrate on your breathing during the last kilometre of the race.
- **concentrate on doing** — Concentrate on keeping your elbows in when you throw the javelin.

### difficult

- **difficult to do** — It is difficult to stay calm when the whole crowd is shouting at you.
- **find sth difficult** — I find chess difficult because I cannot think several moves ahead.
- **find it difficult to do** — He finds it difficult to train in the mornings before school.

### fond

- **fond of sth** — I have always been fond of board games, especially on wet Sunday afternoons.
- **fond of sb** — The team is very fond of the old coach who still comes to every home match.
- **fond of doing** — She is fond of swimming in the outdoor pool, even when it is freezing.

### free

- **free to do** — You are free to use the gym after six, but please wipe the machines afterwards.
- **free from/of sth** — The new rules are meant to keep the sport free from dangerous tackles.
- **free for sth** — Are you free for a game of tennis on Thursday after work?

### interested

- **interested in sth** — He is interested in Formula One and never misses a race on television.
- **interested in doing** — Are you interested in joining the climbing club this term?

### involve

- **involve sth** — The course involves a written test as well as a practical swimming exam.
- **involve doing** — Being captain involves speaking to the referee before every home match.
- **involved in sth** — She has been involved in local athletics since she was nine years old.
- **involved in doing** — He got involved in coaching the under-twelves after he stopped playing.

### listen

- **listen to sth** — Listen to the umpire’s decision and do not argue in the middle of the court.
- **listen to sb** — The players would not listen to the coach, and they lost by a huge margin.

### love

- **love sth** — I love cricket, even when a test match lasts five days and still ends in a draw.
- **love sb** — The fans love that striker because he always stays to sign shirts after the game.
- **love doing** — She loves running along the river before breakfast, whatever the weather.
- **love to do** — He would love to play at Wimbledon before he retires from professional tennis.

### mean

- **mean to do** — I meant to book the court yesterday, but the club’s website was down.
- **it/this means that** — A red card means that the player cannot take part in the next match.
- **it/this means sth** — Missing this training session means a fine, according to the club rules.
- **it/this means doing** — Winning the league this year means beating them twice on their pitch.

### prefer

- **prefer to do (rather than [to] do)** — I prefer to train in the morning rather than run after a full day at work.
- **prefer sth (rather than sth)** — She prefers tennis rather than squash because the court feels less closed in.
- **prefer sth to sth** — Most of the class prefer football to rugby, although a few still enjoy both.
- **prefer doing to doing** — He prefers swimming to jogging because it is easier on his knees.

### stop

- **stop sth** — The referee stopped the match when a dog ran onto the pitch.
- **stop doing** — Please stop shouting at the players; they can hear you from the other end.
- **stop to do** — We stopped to drink some water after the eighth kilometre of the race.
- **stop sb from doing** — Nothing will stop her from competing, not even a twisted ankle.

### sure/certain

- **make sure/certain that** — Make sure that your boots are clean before you walk into the clubhouse.
- **be sure/certain that** — Be certain that you know the offside rule before you argue with the referee.
- **sure/certain to do** — She is sure to win the long jump if she can repeat yesterday’s practice.
- **be sure/certain of sth** — I am not certain of the kick-off time, so I will check the club website.
