# Unit 8 · Vocabulary guide

## Topic vocabulary in contrast

### deny / refuse

**Deny** means to say that something is not true. **Refuse** means to say no to a request, offer or invitation. You deny a claim or an accusation; you refuse to do something, or refuse an offer.

- **deny** — The minister denied the rumour, but every paper printed it the next morning.
- **refuse** — She refused to give the journalist the names of the people in the photographs.

### agree / accept

**Agree** means to have the same opinion, or to say yes to a plan. **Accept** means to take something that is offered, or to believe that something is true.

- **agree** — Most critics agree that the documentary is fair, even if it is rather slow.
- **accept** — He accepted the invitation to appear on the talk show next Friday evening.

### headline / heading

A **headline** is the large title of a newspaper story. A **heading** is a title of a section in a book, article or document.

- **headline** — The headline on the front page claimed that the election had already been decided.
- **heading** — Each heading in the style guide tells you how to write dates and job titles.

### feature / article

A **feature** is a longer, often magazine-style piece, not breaking news. An **article** is any piece of writing in a newspaper or magazine, including news reports.

- **feature** — The Sunday feature on local radio stations ran to four pages of photographs.
- **article** — I read an article about fake news before I posted the link on social media.

### talk show / quiz show / game show

A **talk show** is built around conversation with guests. A **quiz show** tests knowledge. A **game show** is mainly a contest, often with prizes, not a serious interview.

- **talk show** — Last night’s talk show had three writers arguing about the future of newspapers.
- **quiz show** — She won the quiz show by answering every question about 1990s pop music.
- **game show** — The new game show is loud and silly, but a lot of families still watch it.

### announcer / commentator

An **announcer** introduces programmes or reads short messages on radio or TV. A **commentator** describes a live event, such as sport or a ceremony, as it happens.

- **announcer** — The announcer told viewers that the news would start five minutes later than usual.
- **commentator** — The football commentator talked so fast that I could hardly follow the match.

### tabloid / broadsheet

A **tabloid** is a smaller paper, often with short, dramatic stories and big photos. A **broadsheet** is a larger, more serious paper with longer reports. The words still describe style, even when both are read online.

- **tabloid** — The tabloid put a celebrity wedding on the front page and hid the budget story inside.
- **broadsheet** — That broadsheet printed a three-page investigation into how the advert was funded.

### journalist / columnist

A **journalist** reports news or writes for the press in general. A **columnist** writes a regular opinion column, not usually breaking news.

- **journalist** — The journalist spent a month checking every figure before the story went out.
- **columnist** — The columnist is funny, but I do not always agree with her view of the BBC.

### press / media

The **press** is newspapers and magazines, and the people who work for them. The **media** is the wider set of newspapers, TV, radio and the internet.

- **press** — The press were waiting outside the court to photograph the minister as she left.
- **media** — Social media spread the rumour hours before any television channel reported it.

### programme / program

In British English a TV or radio show is a **programme**. **Program** is the usual spelling for computer software. American English uses *program* for both.

- **programme** — There is a science programme on Channel 4 that explains the news without shouting.
- **program** — The newsroom installed a new program to check whether a photo had been edited.

### channel / broadcast

A **channel** is a TV or radio station, or a particular service. To **broadcast** is to send out a programme; a **broadcast** is the programme as it goes out.

- **channel** — Which channel is showing the debate, or is it only streaming online tonight?
- **broadcast** — The live broadcast of the concert reached millions of viewers around the world.

### bulletin / newsflash

A **bulletin** is a short, regular news update. A **newsflash** is a sudden, brief report that interrupts another programme because the news cannot wait.

- **bulletin** — The hourly bulletin said the talks were still going on and nothing had been signed.
- **newsflash** — A newsflash interrupted the film to say that the president had resigned.

## Phrasal verbs

- **bring up** — Please do not bring up the leaked emails until we have checked the source.
- **come on** — The news comes on at ten, after the weather and a short sports report.
- **come out** — Her biography comes out in May, just before the film version is released.
- **fill in** — Fill in your email address at the bottom if you want the daily newsletter.
- **flick through** — He flicked through the magazine and only stopped at the celebrity photographs.
- **go into** — The report goes into the funding of the campaign in far more detail than the TV piece.
- **hand out** — Students handed out leaflets about the student radio station at the main gate.
- **look up** — I looked up the presenter’s name because I was sure I had seen her before.
- **make out** — He made out that he had never seen the article, but his comment was still online.
- **make out** — I could not make out the headline from across the train; the print was too small.
- **make up** — She made up a story about a broken microphone when she missed the live slot.
- **make up** — He makes up little radio plays for his children on the way to school.
- **put forward** — The editor put forward a plan to publish corrections on the front page.
- **see through** — Readers saw through the fake interview as soon as the dates did not match.
- **stand out** — A single honest review stood out among dozens of copied opinions online.
- **turn over** — Turn over and you will find the second half of the interview on page 24.

## Phrases and collocations

### control

- **in control (of sth)** — The director was in control of the live show and decided when to cut to adverts.
- **take control (of sth)** — A larger company took control of the newspaper and changed the front page.
- **under control** — After an hour the rumours were under control and the official statement was online.
- **under the control of sb** — The radio station is under the control of an independent board of editors.
- **out of control** — Comments on the live stream went out of control, and the moderators shut them.

### description

- **give a description of sth** — Please give a description of the photograph so the archive team can file it.
- **give a description of sb** — Witnesses gave a description of the man who stole the reporter’s camera.

### difference

- **make a difference (to sth)** — A single correction on the website made a difference to how readers judged the paper.
- **make a difference (to sb)** — Kind comments from viewers made a difference to the young presenter that night.
- **tell the difference (between)** — Can you tell the difference between the original clip and the edited version?
- **there's no/some/little difference between** — There is little difference between the two headlines once you read the full story.

### granted

- **take sth for granted** — We take free news for granted until a site puts everything behind a paywall.
- **take sb for granted** — The channel took its evening audience for granted and lost them to a new podcast.

### influence

- **influence sth** — Social media can influence the result of a vote if the posts are not checked.
- **influence sb** — Famous reviewers should not influence young writers before the book is even finished.
- **have an influence on sth** — That documentary had a real influence on how people talked about the press.
- **be an influence on sb** — Her first editor was an influence on her, and she still writes in that calm style.

### Internet

- **on the Internet** — The full interview is on the Internet, not in the printed weekend magazine.
- **over the Internet** — They sent the large video files over the Internet instead of using a courier.
- **surf the Internet** — He surfed the Internet for an hour and still could not find a reliable source.

### news

- **in the news** — The singer has been in the news all week because of the leaked messages.
- **on the news** — I saw the fire on the news and then checked the council’s own website.
- **hear the news** — Did you hear the news about the radio station closing at the end of the month?
- **newsflash** — A newsflash crossed the bottom of the screen while the film was still running.
- **newspaper** — She still reads a newspaper on the train, even though the same stories are online.

### place

- **take place** — The press conference will take place at noon in the main hall of the building.
- **in place of** — They ran an old interview in place of the live one when the guest cancelled.
- **at a place** — Photographers were already waiting at the place where the minister was due to speak.

### question

- **ask a question** — Ask a question that cannot be answered with a simple yes or no.
- **answer a question** — He refused to answer a question about who had paid for the advert.
- **question sth** — Readers began to question the figures after a blogger checked the original report.
- **question sb** — Police questioned the photographer for two hours about the leaked pictures.
- **in question** — The article in question has now been taken down from the newspaper’s website.
- **question mark** — There is still a question mark over whether the clip was recorded in this country.

### view

- **have a view** — I have a view on this, but I want to hear the other side before I write.
- **hold a view** — She holds a view that public money should never fund party political adverts.
- **take a view** — The paper took a strong view against the ban and printed three comment pieces.
- **be sb's view that** — It is the editor’s view that corrections should appear on the same page.
- **in my view** — In my view, the headline promised far more than the article actually delivered.
- **in view of** — In view of the complaints, the channel repeated the programme with a warning first.
- **look at the view** — From the studio window you can look at the view of the river and the bridge.
- **see the view** — Tourists came to see the view, then asked if they could watch the live broadcast.
- **view of sth** — The opening shot is a wide view of the city just before the morning news.
- **view from sth/swh** — The view from the roof of the newsroom is better than any stock photograph.

### watch

- **watch sth** — We watched the debate twice to check whether the captions had changed.
- **watch sb** — Millions of people watched her read the statement without looking at her notes.
- **watch (out) for sth/sb** — Watch out for copied articles that use the same mistakes in the same places.
- **keep watch** — Security kept watch at the stage door while the presenter left after the show.

## Word patterns

### according to

- **according to sb** — According to the BBC, the talks will continue through the weekend.

### announce

- **announce sth** — The channel announced a new late-night news programme starting in September.
- **announce sth to sb** — They announced the winner to the studio audience before they told viewers at home.
- **announce that** — The paper announced that it would stop printing a Monday edition next year.

### believe

- **believe sth** — I do not believe a word of that interview; the dates simply do not add up.
- **believe in sth** — She believes in slow journalism, even if it costs the paper some clicks.
- **believe that** — Many readers believe that comments under articles should be checked by a person.
- **believe to be** — The clip is believed to be the original, but nobody has proved it yet.

### comment

- **comment on sth** — The minister refused to comment on the leaked recording during the live interview.
- **make a comment (to sb) about sth** — She made a comment to the producer about the unfair first question.

### confuse

- **confuse sth with sth** — Do not confuse a news report with an advert that is designed to look like news.
- **confuse sb with sb** — Viewers confused the new presenter with her sister, who reads the weather.
- **confused about/by sth** — I was confused by the graphic; the colours did not match the spoken numbers.

### correspond

- **correspond with sth** — The photographs do not correspond with the dates given in the caption.
- **correspond with sb** — He corresponded with readers for years before he ever appeared on television.

### describe

- **describe sth/sb as** — Critics described the documentary as honest, but a little too long.
- **describe sth/sb to sb** — Can you describe the missing camera to security so they know what to look for?

### hear

- **hear sth/sb** — Did you hear the interview, or did you only read the quotes in the paper?
- **hear about sth/sb** — I heard about the closure on the radio while I was driving to work.
- **hear from sb** — We have not heard from the freelance reporter since she arrived in the city.

### inform

- **inform sb that** — Please inform subscribers that tomorrow’s newsletter will arrive an hour later.
- **inform sb about/of sth** — The editor informed the staff of the merger before the press release went out.

### likely

- **likely to do** — The clip is likely to spread online long before any newspaper can check it.
- **likely that** — It is likely that the channel will repeat the debate later this evening.

### persuade

- **persuade sb to do** — They persuaded her to go on the talk show, even though she hates live TV.
- **persuade sb that** — He tried to persuade viewers that the figures came from an independent study.
- **persuade sb of sth** — Nobody could persuade the editor of the need for a front-page apology.

### point

- **point (in) doing** — There is no point in arguing with a headline if you have not read the article.

### send

- **send sb sth** — Send me the original file, not a screenshot from someone else’s timeline.
- **send sth (to sb)** — They sent the statement to every newsroom in the country at the same minute.

### surprise

- **take sb by surprise** — The resignation took the whole newsroom by surprise in the middle of the bulletin.
- **surprised at/by sth** — I was surprised by how calmly she answered the most aggressive question.

### tell

- **tell sb sth** — Tell us the source, or we cannot run the story in tomorrow’s paper.
- **tell sb that** — She told the producer that she would not read an advert as if it were news.
- **tell sb about sth** — He told viewers about the delay and promised a full report after midnight.
- **tell sb about doing** — The guest told us about working night shifts in a small local newsroom.
- **tell sb (not) to do** — The lawyer told them not to name the teenager in any of the reports.
